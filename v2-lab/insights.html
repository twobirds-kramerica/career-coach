Career clarity, in plain language

Three short pieces on the decisions around the job search — the parts a posting analysis cannot answer for you. No quiz, no archetype, no affirmations. Frameworks and sourced facts; you make the calls.

Deciding

What actually matters when evaluating a job offer

An offer letter shows you one number and asks for one signature. The decision underneath it has at least five moving parts, and they trade off against each other. Most regretted job moves come from weighing the visible part (salary) without pricing the invisible ones.

The five parts, priced honestly

  • Total compensation, not base salary. In Canada that means base plus bonus structure, RRSP matching, benefits premiums you stop paying, and vacation days at their cash value. A $78K offer with 4 weeks vacation, full benefits, and 5% RRSP matching can out-earn an $85K offer with none of those. Do the arithmetic before comparing headline numbers.
  • The commute and arrangement, in hours per year. A hybrid role at 2 days a week in an office 45 minutes away costs roughly 150 hours a year — nearly four working weeks. Decide what your hour is worth to you and price it in. Neither answer is wrong; not pricing it is.
  • Growth trajectory. Ask directly in the offer stage: what did the last person in this role move on to? A role that feeds a pipeline is worth more than the same title in a dead-end org chart, and offer stage is when you can still ask.
  • Stability signals. Funding stage, recent layoffs, whether the role is a backfill (someone left — ask why) or new headcount (growth, but less defined). Public information covers most of this; a search on the company name plus "layoffs" takes two minutes.
  • The manager. You interviewed with them; they will shape your daily experience more than the brand on the building. If you left the interviews with reservations about the person you would report to, weigh that reservation heavily — it is the factor least fixable after you sign.

The one question that sorts the rest

Rank what matters most to you right now — pay, stability, growth, flexibility, mission — before you read the offer again. The same offer is strong against one ranking and weak against another. There is no universally good offer; there is only a good offer against your current priorities. (This is the same reason the analysis app asks for your priorities before it scores anything.)

This piece is a decision framework, not financial advice. For Canadian compensation context: Government of Canada Job Bank wage data.

Positioning

Career trajectory beyond years of experience

"How many years do you have?" is becoming a weaker question than it used to be — asked less by good employers, and answered less usefully by strong candidates. Two things are true at once, and both matter to how you position yourself.

What is genuinely changing

Skills-based hiring — screening for demonstrated skills rather than tenure brackets — has real momentum. LinkedIn's own published research reports the share of companies using skills-based hiring practices has grown substantially since 2020, and major staffing firms now publish frameworks for it. Directionally this is well corroborated; treat any single vendor's percentage claims with healthy scepticism, since staffing firms have a commercial interest in the narrative.

The practical consequence: a CV organized around evidence of skills ("built X, shipped Y, measured Z") screens better than one organized purely as a tenure timeline — for career changers, for people with gaps, and for people whose years understate what they can actually do.

What has not changed

Postings still list experience requirements, and many inflate them — analyses of entry-level postings find a large share asking for years of experience an entry-level candidate cannot have, largely because job descriptions get copy-pasted from mid-level templates. The working advice most career professionals give: if you meet the substantive requirements (the skills, the evidence), a stated years number you miss by a year or two should not by itself stop you from applying. If you miss the substantive requirements, no years number saves the application.

How to use this

  1. Judge each posting on its asks, not its years line. That is exactly what the requirements scorecard in the analysis does: met, partial, or missing, with the evidence named.
  2. On the CV, lead with evidence. A skills line plus results bullets in the posting's own vocabulary beats a chronology that makes the reader do the inference.
  3. Never claim a skill you do not have to compensate — screening software gets you shortlisted; the interview is conducted by a person.

Sources: Randstad, 2026 skills-based hiring framework; College Recruiter on the skills-first pivot and requirement inflation (2026). Vendor-published magnitudes are treated as directional, not precise.

Direction

What are you actually optimizing for right now? A framework, not a quiz

Career quizzes will sort you into a type. This is not that. It is four questions to answer for yourself, on paper, in about ten minutes — because a job search run without knowing what it is optimizing for treats every posting as equally worth your evening, and they are not.

The four questions

  1. What does this next role need to buy you? Income now, stability for a season of life, a title that unlocks the role after this one, skills you do not have yet, or time and flexibility. Pick the top two, in order. All five is not an answer.
  2. What kind of work do you keep coming back to? Not what you are qualified for — what you voluntarily do the deep version of. If you want a structured way to think about this, the public-domain O*NET Interest Profiler (built on Holland's six interest areas, developed by the US Department of Labor, free, no account) is the most credible instrument available at no cost — decades of open academic validation, no one selling you a subscription at the end of it.
  3. What are your hard passes? Industries, arrangements, cultures you already know you will not accept regardless of pay. Written down, these save you from re-litigating the same "but the salary though" argument with yourself every week.
  4. What would make you stop the search? Define the acceptable offer before offers exist — the floor salary, the minimum arrangement, the non-negotiables. Deciding this while calm beats deciding it while flattered.

Then let the answers do their job

Your answers become the filter every posting passes through — which is why the analysis app asks for your priorities, hard passes, and salary floor up front, and scores every posting against your stated stakes rather than in a vacuum. Revisit the four questions when circumstances change, not weekly; the point of writing them down is to stop re-deciding them.

The O*NET Interest Profiler and the underlying RIASEC/Holland framework are public-domain, government-published instruments: O*NET Resource Center. This piece is an in-house framework informed by that public research; it reproduces no proprietary assessment.
